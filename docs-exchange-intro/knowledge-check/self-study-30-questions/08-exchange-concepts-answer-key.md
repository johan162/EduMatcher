## Notes on selected answers

- **Q1(C, E):** The company does not receive proceeds from secondary-market trades — only the selling investor does. Bonds, like shares, do trade on secondary markets before maturity so investors can exit early; E has this backwards.
- **Q2(A, D):** "Going short" comes from having *less* inventory than needed to cover a delivery obligation, not more. The Brady Commission's January 1988 report *preceded* and directly motivated NYSE's first circuit breakers later that year — D states the sequence in reverse.
- **Q3(A):** Demutualisation runs from member-owned mutual *to* for-profit public company, not the other way around.
- **Q6(B, D):** Iceberg replenishments receive a fresh timestamp at the back of the queue rather than retaining original priority. A combo order's legs are a single coordinated instruction — leg risk exists precisely *because* the legs are meant to execute together, contrary to D's "entirely independent" framing.
- **Q8(B, D):** With 3,000 shares fully consumed at the first level and only 1,000 of the 4,000-share order remaining, the sweep touches part of the *second* level only — it never reaches the third level at all, so D's "part of the third" is false. Iceberg reserves are explicitly cited as a reason total book depth can understate true available liquidity, contrary to B.
- **Q17(B):** A collar comparing against the *previous close* is the **static** collar; the **dynamic** collar is the one that compares against the most recent trade. The statement mislabels which collar does which.
- **Q18(A):** Reconnection/re-authentication is required after a **kill switch**, not after a mass cancel — a mass cancel leaves the participant's connection fully active throughout.
- **Q16(E):** Regulation SHO requires a locate (confirmed by the broker-dealer accepting the order) before a short sale is accepted; the exchange then enforces the short-sale marking.
- **Q21(E):** Venues act on their own surveillance flags: in the EU and UK they must file Suspicious Transaction and Order Reports (STORs), and US exchanges refer suspected abuse to the SEC or CFTC; none of this waits for a regulator's request.
- **Q24(E):** A single central counter is exactly the design described as creating a single point of failure and a bottleneck, the opposite of E's claim.
- **Q28(E):** Pin risk is sharpest for positions that are *barely* in- or out-of-the-money at settlement — a deep in-the-money option carries essentially no pin risk, since there's no ambiguity about exercise.
- **Q29(D):** FTX is presented as the case where institutional separation *failed* — there was no independent CCP between FTX and its customers, the opposite of D's framing.
